What I do

Between the people who run the operation and the system they run it on

A lot of what goes wrong with supply chain software comes down to the same handful of things: requirements written by someone who's never actually worked the process, configuration decisions made without checking what breaks downstream, or testing that only checks the system against the document instead of against how the floor actually runs. Most of what I do is trying to catch those before they become someone else's problem.

Recent project

Replacing the system a live operation runs on, without stopping the operation

DHL Supply Chain ran its site on JDA RedPrairie. The programme replaced it with Blue Yonder WMS and, alongside that, brought in Nulogy to run co-packing — a platform the site had never touched before. Both had to go live on an operation that was still shipping to real clients every day, so there wasn't really a clean window to hide the cutover in.

The legacy system had years of process baked into it — how stock got received, how locations were named, what actually governed picking — none of it written down anywhere, just lived in how people did the job. Working out which of that should carry over and which shouldn't, then mapping it into a system that does things differently, was most of the gap analysis phase. All while the site kept shipping.

I came into it as a Team Leader who already knew the operation, and ended up covering both sides of the transformation — hands on in the system, and doing the business analysis work. I've tried to keep the two honestly separate below, since they get conflated a lot and they're genuinely different kinds of work.

Integration

Three systems, one flow of stock

Blue Yonder ran the warehouse, Nulogy ran co-pack production, and the only thing connecting them was what passed between the two. Item master data had to land in Nulogy correctly, stock had to move across, and finished goods had to reconcile back into Blue Yonder — all of it as XML messages through DHL Link, the middleware layer in between.

data out of Blue Yonder back into Blue Yonder host and legacy

When a message failed, co-pack production stopped — usually a header field Nulogy didn't recognise, or a message that had gone out of sequence. I'd trace it in the middleware logs, work out what broke, and reprocess it to get the job running again.

How the work ran

From understanding the gap to standing on the floor at go live

01

Gap analysis

This meant comparing how the site actually worked in the legacy system against how Blue Yonder would handle the same process, one process at a time. Every gap forces a decision — change how the operation works to fit the standard product, or configure the product around the operation. I saw both calls made badly elsewhere on the programme: push the operation to change too much and you get resistance on the floor, configure too much around it and you end up with something nobody can upgrade in three years.

This part was business analysis, not build — understanding the current state properly and documenting where the two systems actually diverged, so the team had something real to decide against.

current and future state mappingprocess documentationbusiness analysis
02

Functional design

I contributed to the Functional Design Documents — the thing that turns "the operation needs this" into something a build team can actually configure. A decent FDD has to carry the business requirement, the future state process, the specific system behaviour expected, the integration touchpoints, and the test scenarios it'll get checked against later. Skip any of that and you find out in UAT, which is a worse time to find out.

Sitting in the client working sessions where these got argued over was more useful than the documents themselves, honestly — that's where you actually learn why a requirement exists, not just what it says on paper.

FDD authoringrequirements gatheringdesign workshops
03

Configuration

This was the hands-on part: setting up the master data and control settings everything else in the system depends on. Item master, the product-level record carrying attributes, dimensions and handling rules. Item groups, which decide the storage and pick strategy the system applies to a given product. Pick levels, controlling where in the racking picking happens and what triggers replenishment behind it. Access levels, who's allowed to do what — mostly about stopping mistakes at go live rather than security for its own sake. None of it looks impressive in a workshop, but item groups set up wrong is the kind of mistake that doesn't surface for weeks, once it's touched everything downstream.

I also worked the Nulogy side of the co-pack setup — a platform the site was using for the first time, so there wasn't a "how we used to do it" to fall back on.

04

Integration and data

Item master and stock data moved from Blue Yonder through middleware into Nulogy as XML messages, and when one failed, co-pack production just stopped — quietly, until someone on the floor noticed stock wasn't where it should be. A lot of this phase was tracing those failures back through the middleware logs, working out whether it was the payload or the sequence that broke, and reprocessing the message to get the job moving again.

I used SQL a lot here too, querying job runs directly instead of guessing from the front end when something hadn't completed. That's a habit from the computer science side of my degree, and it's usually still faster than raising a ticket and waiting.

05

Testing and go live

I wrote the UAT scripts and ran them across inbound, outbound and inventory, tying each test back to the requirement it was meant to prove, so nothing got signed off just because it looked fine on the day. Defects went into ALM, got triaged and tracked, and got retested after a fix rather than assumed fixed.

Then there's the part you can't really do from a desk — standing on the floor through go live and hypercare, running real orders on the new system, sorting out whatever came up in the moment, and helping people who'd used the old system for years pick up the new one mid-shift. That's what the recognition below was actually for.

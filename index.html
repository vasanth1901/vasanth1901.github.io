The project

Replacing the system a warehouse runs on, without stopping the warehouse

DHL Supply Chain ran its site on JDA RedPrairie. The programme replaced it with Blue Yonder WMS and, at the same time, introduced Nulogy to run co-packing — a system the site had never used before. Both had to land in an operation that was shipping every day for real clients.

A migration like this isn't a software install. The legacy system had years of accumulated process baked into it — the way stock was received, how locations were named, which rules governed picking. All of that had to be understood, questioned, mapped into a system that does things differently, and then proved to work before anyone could switch over. Meanwhile the warehouse still had orders to ship.

I came into it as a Team Leader who already knew how the floor worked, and ended up working across the transformation: gap analysis and functional design at the front, configuration and integration in the middle, then testing and go-live support at the end. The parts where I was hands-on in the system and the parts where I was doing business analysis are set out honestly below — both mattered, but they're different kinds of work.

How the systems connect

Three systems, one flow of stock

Blue Yonder runs the warehouse. Nulogy runs co-pack production. They aren't the same system, so the two have to stay in step — item master data has to arrive in Nulogy correctly, stock has to move across, and what comes out of co-pack has to reconcile back. That exchange runs through DHL Link, the middleware layer, as XML messages.

data out of Blue Yonder back into Blue Yonder host / legacy

When a message failed, co-pack stopped. Part of my work was tracing those failures in DHL Link, finding what had broken, and reprocessing the message to trigger the job again.

What I did — in order

From understanding the gap to standing on the floor at go-live

01

Gap analysis

Compared how the site actually worked in JDA RedPrairie against how Blue Yonder would handle the same processes. Every gap forces a decision: change the way the operation works to fit the standard system, or configure the system to fit the operation. Getting that call wrong in either direction is expensive — one creates resistance on the floor, the other creates a system nobody can upgrade later.

This was business analysis rather than build work: understanding the current state properly, documenting where the two systems diverged, and giving the team what they needed to make those decisions.

as-is / to-be mappingprocess documentationbusiness analysis
02

Functional design

Contributed to the Functional Design Documents — the bridge between "the operation needs this" and something a build team can actually configure. An FDD has to carry the business requirement, the to-be process, the specific system behaviour expected, the integration touchpoints, and the scenarios it will later be tested against. Vague documents produce systems that pass testing and still fail on the floor.

I sat in the client working sessions and deployment workshops where these decisions were argued out, which is also where you learn why a requirement exists rather than just what it says.

FDD authoringrequirements gatheringdesign workshops
03

Configuration

Hands-on in the system, setting up the master data everything else depends on. Item master — the SKU-level record carrying attributes, dimensions and handling rules; nothing moves correctly without it. Item groups — how products are classified so the system applies the right storage and pick strategy to the right stock. Pick levels — where in the racking picking actually happens, and what triggers replenishment behind it. Access levels — who is permitted to do what, which matters for both security and stopping mistakes at go-live.

These are foundational rather than glamorous. Get item groups wrong and putaway and picking behave wrong everywhere downstream, in ways that only surface weeks later. I also worked the Nulogy side of the co-pack setup, on a platform the site was using for the first time.

item masteritem groupspick levelsaccess levelsNulogy setup
04

Integration & SQL

Systems that don't talk to each other correctly are worse than systems that don't talk at all — because the failure is silent until someone notices stock in one place and not the other. Item master and stock data pass from Blue Yonder through DHL Link into Nulogy as XML messages. When one failed, co-pack production stalled. My work was tracing the failure in the middleware, understanding what in the payload or the sequence had broken it, and reprocessing the message so the job would run.

Alongside that, SQL for investigating job runs that hadn't completed — querying the data directly rather than guessing from the front end. That habit came from the Computer Science side of my background and turned out to be the fastest route to a root cause most of the time.

05

Testing and go-live

Wrote the UAT scripts and then ran them — across inbound, outbound and inventory — tracing each test back to the requirement it was proving so nothing signed off untested. Defects went into ALM, got triaged and tracked, and were retested after fixes rather than assumed resolved.

Then the part that can't be done from a desk: standing on the floor through go-live and hypercare, working real orders on the new system, resolving what came up in the moment and helping staff who had used the old system for years learn the new one mid-shift. This phase is what the site recognition below was awarded for.

The other half

Before and alongside the project I was an operational super user across all three platforms — RedPrairie, Blue Yonder and Nulogy — on live 3PL operations for Lindt and Ritter across the UK and Ireland, including e-commerce order fulfilment.

Why it matters in testing

A test script tells you whether the system does what the document says. Knowing the floor tells you whether that's what the operation actually needs at 6am on a peak Friday.

Why it matters in design

Configuration decisions look abstract in a workshop. They show up later as an operator walking an extra aisle on every pick.

Why it matters at go-live

When something breaks in hypercare, being able to work the process yourself is faster than describing it to someone who can.
